Why the City cares what's in your yard

Every water system in Washington must protect its supply from cross-connections — places where water from a customer's property could flow backward into the public main. It happens when pressure drops in the main: a hydrant opens for a fire, a main breaks, a pump starts. For a moment the pressure in your pipes is higher than the pressure in the street, and anything connected to your plumbing can be siphoned back. The City of Bonney Lake's cross-connection control program exists so that the fertilizer in your irrigation lines, the stagnant water in a fire sprinkler system, or the untreated water from a private well never ends up in a neighbor's glass.

Do you need an assembly? Three questions

Which assembly, and where it goes

The program sorts hazards into two levels. Low-hazard connections — lawn irrigation without chemical injection, most fire sprinklers — get a double check valve assembly (DCVA) or, on some irrigation, a pressure vacuum breaker. High-hazard connections — a private well or pond pump on a property with City water, chemical feed on well water, boilers, commercial and agricultural uses — get a reduced pressure backflow assembly (RPBA), which has a relief port that dumps water if either check fails. Low-hazard assemblies go on the branch that feeds the hazard; high-hazard assemblies go at the service so the whole property is isolated. Every assembly has to be accessible for testing, protected from freezing, and — for an RPBA — given a drain path for its relief port.

The annual test

Washington's Department of Health requires every assembly to be tested after installation, repair or relocation, and once a year after that, by a DOH-certified Backflow Assembly Tester. The test uses a calibrated differential gauge on the assembly's test cocks and takes about twenty minutes. The tester files the report with the City of Bonney Lake Water Department (or Tacoma Water for Tehaleh), and you get a copy. If it fails — debris on a seat, a worn disc, a relief valve that won't open — the assembly is repaired with the manufacturer's kit and re-tested. We're certified testers and licensed plumbers, so one visit covers testing, repair and the filing; we also keep a calendar and remind you a month before the City's deadline.

The side effect: your house is now a closed system

This is the part no one explains. Once a backflow device sits at your meter — whether it's yours or the City's — water can't push back into the main. When your water heater heats a tankful, the water expands by roughly half a gallon, and in a closed system that expansion drives pressure up every heating cycle. The relief valve on the heater vents it, which is why so many Bonney Lake homeowners find a wet floor or a mineral trail under the water heater and assume the valve is bad. The valve is fine; the house needs an expansion tank. Washington's plumbing code requires one on closed systems and inspectors look for it on water heater permits. If the City upgraded your meter in the last few years and your relief valve started dripping afterward, that's the connection.

Winter and the assembly

Irrigation assemblies live outdoors. Left charged through a Plateau freeze, the brass body cracks, and that's a replacement rather than a repair. Each fall we blow down the irrigation and the assembly, or install an insulated enclosure for assemblies that must stay in service — fire sprinklers, commercial supplies. Spring is when we find the ones that weren't winterized.

Commercial and multi-family properties

Restaurants, salons, offices, churches, small apartment buildings and farms along SR 410, Main Street and the valley are treated as high hazard by the program, and most have an RPBA at the service plus additional assemblies on specific equipment — a dishwasher chemical feed, a carbonator, an irrigation branch. The City tracks each assembly by its serial number and expects a test on each one every year. We maintain the list for our commercial plumbing customers, test everything in one visit, repair on the spot where possible, and file every report so no single assembly slips through and triggers a notice.

Selling or buying with a backflow assembly

An untested assembly shows up as an open item with the City and can delay a closing. If you're selling, have it tested and the report filed before listing. If you're buying, ask for the last test report; an assembly that's never been tested is often a sign the irrigation or well connection was done without a permit, which is worth knowing before the price is set.

Got the notice? Here's what to do

Read the deadline. Find the assembly — usually a brass valve pair near the irrigation controller, by the meter, or where the sprinkler riser enters the building. Call (253) 465-7734 with the notice in hand; we test, repair if needed, and file the same day. If you're not sure you have an assembly at all, the City's letter will name the one on record, and we'll find it. Full detail on our backflow preventer page.

When to call

With a notice in hand, call before the deadline and we'll test and file. Without one, call if you have irrigation, a well, a sprinkler system or a business and have never had an assembly tested — the program will find you eventually, and a scheduled test is far easier than a shut-off notice. If your relief valve started dripping after the City worked on your meter, call about an expansion tank; that one isn't a backflow problem, but it's the backflow program's most common side effect.
