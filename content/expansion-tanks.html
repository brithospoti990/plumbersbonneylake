The small part that explains a lot of Bonney Lake plumbing problems

When the City of Bonney Lake Water Department installs a backflow preventer at your meter — part of its cross-connection control program — or when a plumber adds a pressure-reducing valve to tame high pressure, your house becomes a closed system. Water can come in but can't go back out. That's fine until the water heater runs. Heating forty or fifty gallons from 50°F to 120°F expands it by roughly half a gallon, and in a closed system that half-gallon drives pressure up sharply every single heating cycle. The temperature-and-pressure relief valve on the water heater opens to vent it, which is why so many Plateau homeowners find a wet floor or a drip line under the heater and assume the valve is broken.

The valve isn't broken. It's the only thing protecting the tank. Every cycle of pressure spikes also hammers faucet cartridges, toilet fill valves, washing machine hoses and the water heater's own seams. A thermal expansion tank — a small steel tank with a rubber bladder and an air cushion — absorbs that expansion so pressure stays steady. It's one of the least expensive parts in plumbing and one of the most frequently missing.

Do I need an expansion tank? Four quick checks

Signs of thermal expansion in a Bonney Lake home

  • A dripping or weeping relief valve on the water heater, especially right after a shower or a load of laundry. The discharge line may be wet or there may be a puddle or mineral trail under it.
  • Faucets that spit or surge for a second when first opened, then settle.
  • Banging or ticking pipes while the water heater is recovering.
  • Toilet fill valves and faucet cartridges that fail early. Repeated pressure spikes wear the seals.
  • A water heater that fails well before its expected life, often with a split seam or a leak at the top fittings.
  • A pressure gauge on a hose bib that climbs well above normal while the heater runs, then drops when a tap is opened.

Any one of these on a closed system is reason enough. Because the City of Bonney Lake has been adding backflow protection at meters for years, we find homes that were open when the water heater was installed and are closed now — the expansion problem started the day the meter was upgraded, and nobody told the homeowner.

Sizing and pre-charge: the two things installers get wrong

Expansion tanks come in a few sizes keyed to water heater capacity and supply pressure. A two-gallon tank handles most 40- and 50-gallon heaters at normal pressure; larger heaters, higher pressure or recirculation loops call for a bigger tank. Undersizing means the bladder bottoms out and pressure still spikes.

Pre-charge matters more than size. The tank ships with a factory air charge, and if your house's static pressure is higher — which it often is on the Plateau's gravity-fed zones until a pressure-reducing valve is set — the bladder is already compressed before any expansion happens and the tank does nearly nothing. We measure your static pressure with a gauge, adjust the tank's air charge to match before it's filled with water, and note the pressure on the tank for the next person. If your static pressure is above what fixtures are rated for, we'll tell you — a pressure-reducing valve protects the whole house and the expansion tank then gets set to the regulated pressure.

Installation details

  • Mounted on the cold-water supply to the heater, after the shut-off valve, oriented and supported so its weight isn't hanging on a half-inch nipple.
  • A brass tee or dielectric fitting where copper meets the steel tank.
  • Air charge set to measured static pressure; tank labeled with the pressure and date.
  • Relief valve inspected and replaced if it's been weeping long enough to corrode, with the discharge line run correctly.
  • Whole-house pressure checked and a pressure-reducing valve recommended where it's high.
  • On water heater installs, the expansion tank is included — it's part of a code-compliant replacement, not an upsell.
Replacing an old one
An expansion tank that's waterlogged weighs several pounds more than a healthy one and sounds solid when tapped. Replacement takes under an hour and we check the air charge on the new tank before it goes in. If your tank is on an older heater, we check the heater's condition at the same time.

Expansion tanks for other closed systems

The same physics applies anywhere water is heated in a closed loop. Hydronic heating systems, recirculation loops with buffer tanks, and some tankless setups with storage all need expansion control, sized to their own volume and pressure. Small commercial plumbing systems — a restaurant's water heater, a salon, a church kitchen — are nearly always closed and often overlooked. We assess the whole system, not just the water heater.
