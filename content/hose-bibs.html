Why hose bibs fail on the Plateau

A hose bib — a sillcock, an outdoor spigot, whatever you call it — lives a hard life. It's opened and closed hundreds of times a season, often with a wrench or a boot when the handle sticks. A hose hangs off it with a nozzle shut, holding the whole thing under constant pressure. And a few nights every winter, Bonney Lake drops well below freezing while the faucet sits on the coldest side of the house. Older non-frost-free bibs on homes around Lake Tapps and the original city lots freeze solid in those conditions; frost-free models survive only if the hose has been taken off.

The result is a short list of complaints we hear every spring: a faucet that leaks from the handle, one that drips from the spout, one that sprays sideways from a split, and the sneaky one — a frost-free bib that split inside the wall over winter and now leaks into the crawl space or behind the siding only when the faucet is on. That last one is worth checking for right now if you ran a hose last summer and left it on.

Repair or replace?

Standard compression hose bibs are simple and most leaks are repairable: a new washer for a spout drip, new packing or a snug packing nut for a handle leak, a new vacuum breaker if the one on top sprays. If the body is cracked, the seat is pitted beyond dressing, or the bib is soldered to a galvanized stub that's rusting, replacement makes more sense — and we'll replace it with a frost-free model so the next winter isn't a problem.

Frost-free sillcocks are repairable too — the stem, washer and vacuum breaker are all serviceable — unless the stem tube split from freezing. A split tube means replacement. Because the valve seat is at the far end of a long tube inside the wall, that's a job that needs interior or rim-joist access, and we'll find the cleanest route before we open anything.

Frost-free replacement, done so it actually drains

A frost-free sillcock is only frost-free if it's installed correctly. We see plenty around Bonney Lake that were pushed through the wall level or pitched up, so the tube never drains and the "frost-free" faucet freezes like any other. Our replacement includes:

  • The correct stem length for your wall thickness so the valve seat lands in heated space, not in the sheathing.
  • A slight downward pitch to the outside so the tube drains every time it's closed.
  • A built-in or add-on vacuum breaker — required by code to protect your drinking water from hose siphonage.
  • A flange sealed to the siding and the penetration foamed or caulked against air and water intrusion.
  • Secure anchoring so the bib doesn't twist in the wall when a stiff hose is wrestled on and off.
  • An interior shut-off and drain valve where the supply is accessible, so you can winterize completely.
  • A quick look at the supply line it's fed from — if it's an old galvanized stub, we recommend replacing it while the wall is open.

New hose bib installation

If you've been dragging a hundred feet of hose across the yard, adding a hose bib is one of the most satisfying small plumbing jobs there is. We tap the nearest cold line in the crawl space, garage or utility room, run PEX or copper to the new location, and install a frost-free sillcock through the rim joist or wall. Common requests on the Plateau: a bib by the vegetable garden, one on the deck for washing it down, one at the driveway for the cars and the boat, one at an RV pad with a dedicated shut-off, and a hot-and-cold pair in the garage for a utility sink or dog wash. For a detached shop or a garden a long way from the house, we run a buried line with a drain-down valve so it can be winterized.

Fall checklist for every Bonney Lake home
Before the first freeze: take every hose off, drain and coil it; close interior stop valves and open the exterior faucets to drain; put insulated covers on standard bibs; check that the crawl-space supply lines are insulated. Spring: open each faucet with a hose on it and look inside the house for drips. It takes fifteen minutes and prevents most of the hose bib calls we get.
