Why hot water takes so long on the Plateau's bigger homes

The wait for hot water has nothing to do with the water heater's power and everything to do with pipe. Every foot between the heater and the tap holds water that cooled off since the last use, and all of it has to be pushed out before hot arrives. In a compact 1970s rambler near the lake that's a few seconds. In a two-story Tehaleh, Sky Island or Prairie Ridge home with the heater in the garage and the primary bath at the far end of the second floor, it's commonly sixty to ninety seconds and a gallon or more every time. Three-quarter-inch trunk lines, common in newer construction, hold more than half-inch lines and make it worse.

A recirculation system keeps that pipe charged with hot water so the wait disappears. Use the calculator to see what your current wait is costing you.

Two ways to build it

There are two recirculation designs, and the right one is decided by the plumbing already in your walls.

Dedicated return loop

The hot line runs out to the farthest fixture and a separate return line comes back to the water heater. A small pump on the return keeps water moving slowly through the loop, so every fixture along it has hot water within a second or two. This is the most efficient and comfortable design — no effect on the cold side, lowest heat loss when insulated — and it's increasingly standard in new construction in Tehaleh and the newer Bonney Lake subdivisions. If your home has a capped three-quarter-inch line near the heater labeled "recirc," you already have the return; we add the pump, check valve and control.

Comfort (crossover) system

For homes without a return line, a thermostatic crossover valve mounts under the farthest sink and connects the hot and cold supplies. A pump at the heater pushes hot water out through the hot line; cooled water crosses over into the cold line and returns to the heater that way. No new pipe through finished walls. The trade-off is that the cold tap at that sink runs lukewarm for a moment after a cycle, and the cold-side pipe carries a little heat. Run on a timer, a motion sensor or a push button, it's a very good solution for most existing Bonney Lake homes.

Controls: the part that decides your energy bill

A pump that runs all day keeps the pipes hot all day, and the water heater pays to replace that heat constantly. The savings — and most of the comfort — come from running it only when you're about to use hot water. We install and program:

  • Timers — on for the morning rush and the evening, off overnight and midday. The simplest option and the most common.
  • Aquastat — a thermostat on the return that shuts the pump off once the loop is hot, so it runs minutes per hour rather than continuously.
  • Demand buttons and motion sensors — press a button or walk into the bathroom and the pump runs for a couple of minutes. The most efficient design; nothing runs when nobody's home.
  • Smart pumps — learn your pattern and schedule themselves, with app control. Useful for irregular routines.

Whatever the control, we insulate the hot trunk and the return where they're accessible. Uninsulated recirculating pipe in a crawl space or garage loses heat continuously, and insulation is the cheapest efficiency upgrade in the whole job.

Recirculation with tankless and heat pump water heaters

Tankless units need a minimum flow before they fire, and some will short-cycle or throw an error if a recirculation pump is matched poorly. Several manufacturers now build recirculation modes into their units, with a small internal pump and a schedule, and those pair well with a dedicated return loop. For others we install an external pump sized to the heater's minimum flow and a buffer tank where needed. Heat pump water heaters recover slowly, so with those we favor demand-based controls that don't drain the tank keeping pipes warm all day. If you're planning a tankless or water heater replacement, telling us about the long wait at the far bathroom at the same time lets us design both together and saves a second visit.

What recirculation won't fix
A shower that starts hot, then goes cold, then hot again is a cross-connection or a failed mixing valve, not a recirculation problem. Hot water that never gets hot enough is a water heater or thermostat issue. We diagnose before we quote so you don't buy a pump for a problem it can't solve.
