An honest assessment before anything is ordered

Walk-in bathtubs are heavily advertised, and the ads skip the plumbing. A walk-in tub only works well if the house can fill it fast with enough hot water, drain it quickly, and get the bather in and out safely. In many Bonney Lake homes with a 40-gallon water heater and a slow 1½-inch drain, a walk-in tub as sold is a disappointing experience. In others, with the right heater and drain, it's life-changing. Use the assessment below, and then let us measure the two numbers that decide it: your water heater capacity and your drain.

Walk-in tub or walk-in shower?

What makes a walk-in tub installation work

Hot water capacity

This is the make-or-break. A walk-in tub fills with the door closed and the bather inside, so a tub that holds 60 gallons needs most of that as hot water, delivered at once. A 40-gallon water heater, common in Bonney Lake homes, can't do it. We measure the heater's size and recovery against the tub's volume and, where it's short, include a larger tank or a tankless unit in the plan. Doing this honestly up front is the main thing that separates a tub people love from one they stop using.

Fill and drain speed

Waiting in an empty tub while it fills, and in a full one while it drains, is the walk-in tub's weak point. A fast-fill faucet needs good water pressure and supply size; a fast-drain tub needs a drain line that can take the flow — often a 2-inch line rather than the 1½-inch the old tub used. We measure pressure at the house and check the drain before recommending a model.

Access and door swing

Inward-swinging doors are watertight by design but need clearance inside the tub; outward-swinging doors need floor space. The step height, the seat height and the position of grab bars are set for the person who'll use it, not for a brochure.

Power

Heated seats, jets and inline heaters need a dedicated GFCI circuit near the tub. We coordinate a licensed electrician so plumbing and electrical finish together.

The alternative we often recommend: the low-threshold shower

For a lot of Plateau households, a walk-in shower with a two-inch or curbless threshold, a fold-down bench, a hand shower on a slide bar, a thermostatic anti-scald valve and grab bars set into solid backing does more for daily safety than a tub, at lower cost and with no waiting. It's also what works for wheelchair and walker users, who can't step into a walk-in tub at all. We build these as tub-to-shower conversions, and we'll show you both options side by side with the plumbing requirements of each.

Installing a tub you've already bought

If a national walk-in tub company has sold you a unit, we'll install it — and we'll check its plumbing requirements against your house first. The common surprises are a water heater that's too small, a drain that's too slow, and a filler faucet that needs more flow than the bathroom's half-inch supply can give. All three are solvable, and knowing before installation day is far cheaper than finding out after. Our installation includes the waste-and-overflow, drain upgrade if needed, the valve or filler, hand shower, supply lines, leveling and sealing, the plumbing permit and inspection, and coordination with the electrician for any powered features.

Small upgrades that make the biggest difference
Before a tub or a conversion, three things cost little and matter a lot: an anti-scald valve so the water can't spike hot; grab bars into solid backing (not drywall anchors); and a comfort-height toilet. We install all three on the same visit. See toilet replacement and fixture installation.
