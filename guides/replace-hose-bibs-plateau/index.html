When to Replace Hose Bibs on the Plateau (and How to Keep Them Alive)

By Ashino Thomas, founder · October 6, 2026

Outdoor faucets are the most-abused valves on a house and the first thing a Plateau freeze breaks. Most of the damage is preventable in fifteen minutes each fall — and most of the rest is a quick replacement, if you catch it before spring.

Walk the house before the first freeze

Bonney Lake, Lake Tapps and the foothill towns get a handful of hard freezes most winters — not many, but enough. Every outdoor faucet on the property should get a thirty-second look in October. Use the checklist below; it saves your progress on this device so you can finish it over a weekend.

0 of 8 doneStart with the hoses — it's the one that prevents the most damage.

How outdoor faucets fail here

There are two kinds of hose bib on Plateau houses. The standard compression bib closes right at the exterior wall, so the body outside the wall holds water — and freezes. The frost-free sillcock has a long stem that closes the valve inside the heated wall; the tube between the seat and the spout drains after each use, so there's nothing to freeze. Frost-free bibs became standard on new construction around Bonney Lake in the 1990s, which means the older homes on the original lake lots and in Prairie Ridge, Sumner and Buckley still carry standard bibs, and the newer ones in Tehaleh and Lakeland Hills have frost-free bibs that are only frost-free when no hose is attached.

The failure pattern follows: standard bibs split or crack their bodies in a freeze, usually with a visible leak the next time they're turned on. Frost-free bibs with a hose left on split the stem tube inside the wall; the leak appears inside the crawl space or behind the siding in spring, when the faucet is first used — sometimes weeks after the damage, which is why spring water-damage calls so often trace back to a hose bib.

Repair or replace?

  • Drip from the spout when closed — a worn washer or seat. Repairable on most bibs; if the seat is pitted beyond dressing, replace.
  • Leak from the handle when open — packing. Tighten the packing nut a quarter turn; if it persists, new packing. Repair.
  • Spray from the top — the vacuum breaker. Replaceable part.
  • Cracked body, or a frost-free stem that's split — replacement, and the chance to upgrade a standard bib to a frost-free one pitched correctly through the wall.
  • Bib soldered to a rusty galvanized stub — replace the stub too, while the wall is open.

Our rule: a standard bib that's leaking gets replaced with a frost-free one rather than repaired, because the repair saves little and the freeze risk stays. A frost-free bib that's leaking gets repaired unless the tube is split.

Installing a frost-free bib so it actually works

We see plenty of frost-free sillcocks around Bonney Lake that freeze every year because they were installed level or pitched up, so the tube never drains. A correct installation uses the right stem length for the wall thickness so the seat lands in heated space, a slight downward pitch to the outside, a built-in or add-on vacuum breaker (required by Washington code), a sealed flange at the siding, solid anchoring so the bib doesn't twist when a stiff hose is wrestled on, and ideally an interior shut-off and drain so the line can be winterized completely. Full details on our hose bib page.

Adding faucets where you need them

If you drag a long hose across the yard every summer, a new frost-free bib by the garden, the driveway or the dock is one of the cheapest quality-of-life upgrades on a property. We tap the nearest cold line in the crawl space or garage, run PEX to the new spot, and install the sillcock through the rim joist with a vacuum breaker and a drain-down valve inside. On rural properties toward Buckley and South Prairie, a frost-free yard hydrant — the tall, lever-handled kind that drains below the frost line — is the right choice for animals, gardens and shops away from the house.

Hose bibs and water pressure

A hose bib is where we put the gauge to measure a house's pressure, and the reading explains a lot of outdoor-faucet complaints. A bib that spits or that leaks only when the water heater is running points to thermal expansion in a closed system. One that's lost pressure may be downstream of a corroded galvanized stub, or on a long run that was never upsized. If several outdoor faucets are weak at once, the problem is the house, not the faucets — see the water pressure guide.

Where it goes wrong most: Lake Tapps and the rural edges

Waterfront homes on Lake Tapps have more outdoor faucets than most — dock, deck, boat wash, garden — and the lake-facing side is the cold side. Rural properties in Buckley, South Prairie and the Carbon River valley add yard hydrants, shop lines and stock-tank supplies that sit fully exposed. For both, the October walk-around is the whole game. If you'd rather we did it, our fall winterizing visit covers every outdoor faucet, the irrigation backflow, the crawl-space lines and the water heater flush in one stop. Call (253) 465-7734.

When to call

A bib that dripped this summer, a standard bib on a lake-facing wall, a frost-free faucet that froze last winter, or a wet spot in the crawl space that only appears when the outdoor faucet runs — any of these is worth a visit before the first freeze, and all of them are quick jobs in October and expensive ones in January. Frequently asked questions

How do I know if my hose bib is frost-free?

A frost-free sillcock has a long body — the handle sits at the wall, but the valve seat is 8 to 14 inches inside. Standard bibs are short and close right at the wall. Looking inside the crawl space or garage at the pipe behind it tells you which.

My frost-free faucet froze anyway. Why?

Almost always a hose left attached. It traps water in the spout, which freezes back into the stem tube and splits it. The leak appears inside the wall the first time you use it in spring.

Is a dripping outdoor faucet urgent?

Not urgent, but worth fixing before winter. A drip means the washer or seat is worn, and a worn bib is the one most likely to split in a freeze.
