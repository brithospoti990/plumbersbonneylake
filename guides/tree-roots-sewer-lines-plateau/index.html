Root Intrusion and the Plateau's Mature Trees

By Ashino Thomas, founder · October 6, 2026

The Douglas firs and cedars that make Bonney Lake, Prairie Ridge and Lake Tapps lots beautiful were saplings when the houses went in. Fifty years later they tower over fifty-year-old sewer pipe, and their roots have found every joint. Here's how to know if yours have.

Estimate your risk

Root intrusion isn't random. It tracks three things: the pipe's age and material, how close large trees are to its path, and whether the soil stays moist — which on the Plateau's glacial till, it does. Enter what you know; the estimate tells you whether a camera inspection should come before the next cleaning.

Root intrusion risk estimator

Why the Plateau is root country

Three things line up here. First, the trees: Douglas fir, western red cedar and bigleaf maple are large, thirsty, and send roots long distances through shallow soil. Second, the soil: glacial till drains slowly, so the zone around a sewer trench stays moist, and a leaking joint stays moist longest. Third, the pipe: homes around Lake Tapps, the original Bonney Lake lots, Prairie Ridge, downtown Sumner, Orting and Buckley were plumbed with clay, concrete, cast iron or Orangeburg, all of which have joints every few feet and all of which leak a little as they age. A root doesn't need a hole; it needs a damp joint, and a fine filament finds its way in. Once inside, it grows toward the water, thickens, and becomes a mat that catches paper and solids until the line backs up.

Signs you have roots now

  • The main line backed up, was cabled, and backed up again within a year or two.
  • Drains throughout the house are slow at once, or gurgle when another fixture runs.
  • A strip of grass along the sewer's path stays greener or soggier than the rest of the yard.
  • The cabling technician pulled back root fragments — they'll usually tell you.
  • A sewer smell outside near the line's path or at the cleanout.

Any one of these means a camera inspection will pay for itself. It shows exactly where the entries are, how many, and whether the pipe between them is sound.

The honest options

Cutting. A root-cutting head on the cable clears the mat. It's the right first step and the right maintenance for a line you plan to fix later. On its own it's a subscription: roots return through the same joint.

Root-inhibiting treatments. Foaming herbicides applied to the line slow regrowth. Useful between cuttings; not a repair.

Spot repair. One bad joint, located from the surface, dug and replaced with PVC. The least invasive fix when the rest of the line is sound.

Lining. A cured-in-place liner creates a new jointless pipe inside the old one — no trench, and roots have nothing to enter. Needs a round, mostly intact host pipe; can't fix a belly.

Bursting or replacement. For collapsed, badly offset or Orangeburg pipe. Two pits or a trench, new pipe at grade, cleanouts added.

The camera decides which. We'll show you the video, mark the spot and quote the option that fits — which, if the pipe is mostly sound, is usually the cheapest one.

Roots in drain lines inside the house?

Roots can't reach the drains inside a house, but a root-choked lateral produces symptoms that look like interior problems: a kitchen sink that drains slowly, a tub that holds water, a toilet that flushes weakly. The tell is that several fixtures misbehave at once, or that the lowest drain in the house — a floor drain, a basement shower — is where water appears. One slow fixture is a local clog; everything slow is the lateral. Our drain cleaning page has a quick checker.

Planting near the line

If you're landscaping, the sewer lateral runs from the cleanout by the house to the street in a fairly straight line, usually under the lawn rather than the driveway. Keep new firs, cedars, maples, willows and poplars at least thirty feet from it; fruit trees and ornamentals are gentler. A camera and locator can mark the line's exact path on the surface before you plant, which is a short visit and far cheaper than finding it later with a backhoe.

Cleanouts: the small upgrade that changes everything

Many older Plateau homes have no accessible cleanout on the lateral — the only way in is through a roof vent or by pulling a toilet. That makes every root cutting slower and messier than it needs to be and makes a camera inspection harder. Adding a two-way cleanout near the foundation is a short excavation, and from then on every cleaning, camera and future repair starts at a capped pipe in the yard instead of inside the house. We add one on almost every sewer repair we do, and we'll quote it on its own for homes that just need a better way in.

Buying or selling a Plateau home?

A sewer scope before closing is standard advice for any pre-1990 house, and on the Plateau we'd extend it to any lot with a big fir or cedar between the house and the street, regardless of age. The video and a written summary cost far less than a surprise in the first winter. Sellers can get ahead of it the same way. When to call

If the main has backed up more than once, if drains throughout the house are slow, or if the risk estimator came back moderate or high, call for a camera inspection. It takes under an hour, you see the video, and we mark any problem spot on the surface. From there the quote is for exactly what the pipe needs — a cutting on your schedule, a spot repair, a liner, or a replacement — and nothing more. For a backup happening right now, stop running water and see emergency plumbing.

Frequently asked questions

How do roots get into a sewer pipe?

Through joints and cracks. Older clay, concrete and cast-iron laterals have a joint every few feet, and each one leaks a little moisture into the soil. Roots follow the moisture to the joint, enter as hair-fine filaments, and grow into a mat that catches everything.

Will cutting the roots fix it?

For a while — months to a couple of years. Cutting removes the mat but leaves the opening, so roots return. Lining or replacing the affected section removes the entry point.

Should I cut down the tree?

Rarely necessary. A sound pipe doesn't attract roots. Fixing the pipe is cheaper than removing a mature fir and keeps the lot's character.
