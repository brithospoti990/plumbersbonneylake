Plumbing around the lake

Lake Tapps is a reservoir, not a natural lake, and the community around it grew up in waves: summer cabins on the shoreline from the 1950s and 60s, year-round homes and split-levels through the 70s and 80s on the east and north shores, Tapps Island's planned lots, and the North Tapps and Lakeland Hills subdivisions from the 1990s onward. That history is written in the pipes. A waterfront home that started as a cabin usually has had three generations of plumbing added to it — galvanized steel at the core, copper from the first remodel, PEX from the last — and the joints between those eras are where it leaks. The newer subdivisions are cleaner but bring their own patterns: closed systems with thermal expansion problems, long hot-water runs, and builder-grade fixtures reaching the end of their life all at once.

We're based on 198th Ave E in Bonney Lake, a few minutes from the south shore and under fifteen from the north end by way of Sumner-Tapps Highway. For an emergency — a burst pipe, no hot water, a sewer backup — that proximity is the difference between a wet crawl space and a flooded one. For scheduled work, it means we can look at a job in the morning and do it in the afternoon.

Water in Lake Tapps homes

Most of the Lake Tapps community is served by the City of Bonney Lake Water Department, drawing on the City's springs and wells with a Tacoma Water supplement, while parts of the north end fall under other purveyors — check your bill. It's moderately hard groundwater with some sediment, chlorinated for distribution, and it leaves the scale on fixtures and water heaters that every Plateau home knows. A handful of older lakeside properties are still on private wells, which bring iron and the occasional sulfur smell into the picture. The lake itself is not a drinking source for homes; irrigation pumps drawing from it need a backflow assembly on the house side so lake water and drinking water never meet. See our water softener, filtration and backflow pages.

Homes and what we see in them

Waterfront homes sit on slopes, and their plumbing runs through crawl spaces that are damp, cold and often half-forgotten. Supply lines freeze there in January; drain lines slope the wrong way after decades of settling; and sump and ejector pumps in lower-level bathrooms fail quietly. Older homes on the original lots frequently have galvanized supply lines that have narrowed to a trickle — when a Lake Tapps homeowner tells us the shower loses pressure when the kitchen tap runs, that's usually the diagnosis, and a repipe is the fix. The 1980s split-levels have copper that's generally sound, with polybutylene appearing in a few late-80s builds. North Tapps and Lakeland Hills homes from the 1990s and 2000s are mostly PEX or copper and are now reaching the age where the original water heater, disposal and fill valves all fail within a few years of each other.

A year of plumbing in Lake Tapps

Late fall. The first hard freeze is when we get the calls that could have been avoided: disconnect hoses, drain outbuilding lines, and make sure exposed supply runs are insulated. Lake Tapps homes with sloped lakeshore lots, damp crawl spaces, glacial till should treat this as a yearly appointment, not a one-time fix.

Winter. Burst pipes, frozen hose bibs and water heaters that quit on the coldest morning. We dispatch emergencies from Bonney Lake — 5–15 minutes from our bonney lake office — ahead of all scheduled work, and we walk you through the shut-off on the phone while the truck is moving.

Spring. The leaks winter caused show up when outdoor faucets are first used: a split frost-free stem leaking inside the wall, a crawl-space line weeping at a fitting. It is also sewer season — saturated ground and active roots mean the laterals that are going to back up this year usually do it between March and May. Frozen or split hose bibs and roots in sewer laterals are the two calls we schedule most in Lake Tapps at this time of year.

Summer. Irrigation goes on, which means backflow assembly tests are due and the water heater finally gets the flush it needed. It is the best season for planned work — repipes, remodel rough-ins, water heater replacements before the fall rush — because walls dry fast and the crawl space is at its driest.

Permits, inspections and who owns what

Lake Tapps is mostly unincorporated Pierce County, with the Bonney Lake and Auburn city limits touching its shores, so permits go through Pierce County Planning and Public Works or the relevant city depending on your address. We pull the permit in our name, meet the inspector, and hand you the final — the paperwork matters when you sell. On the utility side, the water purveyor owns the main and the meter; everything from the meter into the house is yours, and that is what we repair. The same split applies to sewer: the lateral from the house to the main is the homeowner's, and we confirm the exact connection point with the utility before quoting a line repair.

Questions from Lake Tapps homeowners

Do you serve the whole Lake Tapps shoreline?

Yes — Tapps Island, the east and north shores, North Tapps, Lakeland Hills and the Bonney Lake side. All of it is within a short drive of our office.

Who provides water in Lake Tapps?

Most of the community is served by the City of Bonney Lake Water Department; some northern areas are on other purveyors and a few older lakeside lots have private wells. Your utility bill tells you which, and we adjust the equipment we recommend accordingly.

My waterfront home loses pressure when two taps run. Why?

Almost always narrowed galvanized supply pipe from the original build. A repipe to PEX restores full flow; we confirm with a pressure test before quoting.

Can I use lake water for irrigation?

With a permit and a backflow assembly on the house side, so the pump line can never cross-connect with drinking water. We install and test the assembly.

How fast can you get to Lake Tapps in an emergency?

Usually within the hour on a normal day, because we dispatch from Bonney Lake rather than Seattle or Tacoma.
