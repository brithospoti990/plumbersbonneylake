Water Line Repair & Replacement in Bonney Lake, WA

A water bill that doubled, a soggy patch in the lawn that never dries, a meter that spins with every tap closed — the main water line between the meter and the house is leaking. We locate it, repair the section or replace the line, and get your pressure back.

Is water leaving the line? A ten-minute test

Before anyone digs, confirm there's a leak and get a sense of its size. Your water meter — in the box at the edge of the property, usually near the street — measures everything that passes it. With every tap, appliance, irrigation zone and ice maker off, the meter should sit perfectly still. Use the tool below to turn two readings into a leak rate.

Meter leak test

Turn everything off. Read the meter (the full number, including decimals or the small dial). Wait, then read again.

If the meter moved, close the main shut-off at the house and read again: still moving means the leak is between the meter and the house; stopped means it's inside.

Where water lines fail around Bonney Lake

The service line runs underground from the City's meter to your house, and on the Plateau it's buried in glacial till — dense, rocky, and prone to shifting as it saturates and dries through the year. Four failure patterns account for most of our calls:

  • Galvanized steel lines in the oldest homes around the lake rust from the inside, narrowing until pressure drops, then pinhole from the outside. They're past their life and we replace them rather than patch them.
  • Thin-wall polyethylene from the 1970s–90s gets punctured by rocks in the backfill or cracks at a fitting. Spot repairs work if the rest of the line tests sound.
  • Copper lasts decades but can pit in acidic soil or fail at a fitting that was never sleeved through the foundation.
  • Root and settlement damage at the meter connection or where the line enters the house — the two points that move the most.

Inside the house, leaks show up in crawl spaces (a wet vapor barrier, a dripping fitting) and under slabs (a warm spot on the floor, the sound of water, a meter that won't stop). Both are findable without guesswork.

Finding the leak before digging

A leak that shows itself as a wet spot is easy. Most don't. Water on the Plateau tends to follow the pipe trench or drain down through the till, so the surface stays dry while thousands of gallons a month disappear. We use acoustic listening equipment on the line and at the meter and house ends, pressure testing to isolate sections, and tracer and ground-penetrating methods when the line's path isn't known. The result is a marked spot and a plan: one excavation for a spot repair, or a route for a replacement. We'll also check the pressure-reducing valve and the house's static pressure — a failed PRV is a common reason a line that lasted 40 years suddenly starts leaking.

Repair vs. replace

If the line is a sound material with one failure — a rock puncture, a bad coupling at the meter, a crack at the foundation sleeve — a spot repair with a proper burial-rated fitting is the right call, and we pressure-test the whole run afterward to be sure it was the only one. If it's galvanized, if it's thin polyethylene with a second leak in a few years, or if pressure has been dropping for a while, replacement is the honest recommendation. A new one-inch Type K copper or PE line, properly bedded in sand and sleeved through the foundation, with a new main shut-off and, where needed, a pressure-reducing valve, restores the flow your house was designed for and ends the problem for good.

Replacement doesn't have to mean a trench the length of the yard. Where the old line's path is usable we can pull new pipe through; where it crosses a driveway or runs under a mature tree we often choose a new route and leave the old line abandoned in place. Either way, we coordinate the shut-off at the meter with the City of Bonney Lake Water Department, pull the permit and schedule the inspection.

Low pressure without a leak?
If the meter test shows nothing moving but pressure is poor, the likely causes are a narrowed galvanized line, a failing pressure-reducing valve, a partly closed main valve, or scale in the filter or softener. We measure pressure at the hose bib and the house to find which.

Questions Bonney Lake homeowners ask about water line repair

How do I know if my water line is leaking?

Close every tap and appliance, then watch the meter at the street: if the small dial or digits move, water is flowing somewhere. A higher-than-usual City of Bonney Lake bill, a wet spot in the yard, unexplained low pressure, or the sound of running water with everything off are the other signs.

Who is responsible — me or the City?

The City of Bonney Lake Water Department owns the main and the service line up to and including the meter. Everything from the meter to the house is the homeowner's. On Tacoma Water (Tehaleh) the split is the same. We repair the homeowner's side.

Can you repair just the leaking section?

Often yes, when the line is otherwise sound — a single failed joint or a rock-punctured spot. When the line is old galvanized steel or thin polyethylene with multiple failures, replacing the whole run is cheaper than chasing leaks.

Do you dig up the whole yard?

Not usually. We locate the leak first, then open the ground only there. For full replacements we can often pull new pipe through the old path with minimal trenching, or route a new line to avoid driveways and mature trees.

What pipe do you use for a new water line?

Type K copper or PE (polyethylene) rated for burial, sized for the house's demand — typically one inch for modern homes. Both outlast the old galvanized and thin-wall plastic lines we replace.

Is a permit required?

Yes. Replacing or repairing the service line between the meter and the house is permitted work in Bonney Lake and Pierce County, and the City will want to see the connection at the meter. We handle it.

What about leaks inside the house — under a slab or in a crawl space?

We find and repair those too. Crawl-space leaks are straightforward; slab leaks need electronic location and either a spot repair through the slab or a reroute overhead, which we'll explain and quote both ways.
